# Research protocol

Use this when the task is research-heavy, historical, policy-oriented, or comparison-heavy.

## Step 1: Define the claim before collecting evidence

Write the claim in a falsifiable form. Avoid topics such as “What can we learn from X?” until you have narrower questions.

## Step 2: Separate layers

Maintain four columns:

| Layer | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fact | What the source directly records |
| Source interpretation | What the author claims the fact means |
| Our interpretation | What we infer |
| Modern transfer | What might apply today |

Never collapse the four.

## Step 3: Build an evidence ladder

Prefer original texts, archival materials, official records, datasets and contemporaneous documents. Use secondary scholarship to locate disputes and context.

For each important claim, record:

- source;
- date;
- author / institution;
- proximity to event;
- possible incentives or bias;
- whether another independent source confirms it.

## Step 4: Search for disconfirming evidence

For the current best explanation, ask:

- What observation would make this wrong?
- Which source would an informed critic cite?
- Are we selecting only examples that fit the story?

## Step 5: Compare on equal units

Do not compare a mature company with a startup, a national statistic with a city statistic, or a wartime policy with a peacetime policy without explicitly adjusting for context.

## Step 6: State confidence

Use qualitative confidence when precision is not justified:

- High: multiple independent high-quality sources agree.
- Medium: good evidence exists but interpretation is contested or incomplete.
- Low: evidence is sparse, indirect or highly dependent on one source.

## Step 7: End with what would change the conclusion

Every serious research output should include at least one “reversal condition”: a future fact or source that would cause a different conclusion.
